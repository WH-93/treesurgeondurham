Storm damage to trees is the most common emergency call we get, and the most common place people make things worse. Here is the order of operations.

## First: make it safe, not tidy

1. **Keep people and pets away** from the damaged tree and anything it is resting on. A split limb can settle or fall further at any time.
2. **Do not walk under it** to take photos or inspect it, and do not let children play near it.
3. **If it is on a building, car or power line, do not try to move it.** If a power line is involved, call the electricity network first (in County Durham that is Northern Powergrid, 105).
4. **If the tree is blocking a road**, report it to Durham County Council's highways team. It is usually their responsibility to clear the carriageway.
5. **Only then** think about the tree itself.

## What not to do

- **Do not pull a fallen tree with a car or 4x4.** The tree can spring back, and the tension is dangerous. This is how people get hurt after storms.
- **Do not cut the trunk while it is under load.** A tree resting on a roof or fence is under enormous tension. Cutting the wrong side makes it kick, roll or drop. This is genuinely dangerous and is the main reason we use rigging.
- **Do not cut branches that are touching a power line**, ever. That is a network job.
- **Do not accept the first "it has to come down" verdict** if the tree is not actually dangerous. We assess damaged trees properly and will tell you if it can be saved with a crown reduction or cable work.

## What the emergency call looks like

When you call about storm damage, tell us:

- Whether the tree or a branch is on a building, car or path
- Whether anything is blocking access
- Whether any power lines are involved
- Roughly how big the tree or limb is

We prioritise callouts where the tree is on or near something. A limb hanging in the canopy over a drive is dealt with before a tree that has fallen into an open garden with nothing under it.

## After the emergency

- **Check for other damage.** A tree that lost one limb in the storm may have cracked other limbs. We look for splits, bark damage and leaning while we are on site.
- **Assess whether the tree can be kept.** Not every storm-damaged tree needs to come down. If the structural damage is limited, a crown reduction and deadwooding can often keep a tree safe for years.
- **Think about prevention.** Trees with heavy canopies, dense crowns or included bark (V-shaped forks) are the ones that fail in storms. Regular crown reduction and deadwooding is cheaper than the removal after a storm.

## Insurance

If the tree has damaged your property, your buildings or contents insurance may cover the removal. We provide a written report of what happened and what we did, which makes claims straightforward. If a neighbour's tree has fallen onto your property, it is usually your insurance that deals with it, not theirs.
